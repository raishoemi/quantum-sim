## Werner Heisenberg
In May 1925, Werner Heisenberg asked his mentor Max Born for a 14-day leave of absence.
To recover, he went to Heligonald (an island in the North Sea).
There, he formulated the mathematically consistent theory of quantum mechanics.

At the forefront of his mind was the prevailing model of the structure of the atom (Bohr Model)
The model consists of electrons orbiting the nucleus, like planets around the sun.
The model was known to be inadequate, even to Bohr himself. It worked for Hydrogen but not for many other atoms.

Heisenberg thought to himself, what if the core issue is the "orbit" assumption? we never observed the electrons orbit.
So he focused on observable quantities - such as the Atomic Spectra: the observed signatures of atoms.


>So far we had always believed in the doctorine of Democritus, which can be summarized by: `in the beginning was the particle`.
>We had assumed that visible matter was composed of smaller units, which Democritus had called `atoms` and which modern physics call `elemntary particles`.
>But perhaps this entire approach has been mistaken.
> -Werner Heisenberg

2,500 years ago, a Greek (Democritus) and Indian (Kanada) philosophers both independently considered if objects can be divided into smaller and smaller objects,
until reaching something fundemental. In greek it was called "Atomos", and in Sanskrit "Anu".

In 1809, John Dalton proposed that any objects is made up of indivisble tiny objects called Atoms.
Dalton's theory was more developed: he said every atoms was a sphere with a certain mass, and each element consisted purely of identical atoms of that element.
For instance, oxygen was made of one type of atom, helium from another and so forth.
Moreover, atoms can combine to form new compounds.
Dalton's theory could explain all the laws of chemistry at the time.
It remained the standard model of the atom for almost 100 years.

In 1897, J. J. Thompson came up with a new model.
He was interested in studying the behavior of something called "Cathode Rays":
When two plates, one positively charged (Anode) and one negatively charged (Cathode) were separated by a small distance, a stream of particles moved from the negative to the positive plate.
It was unclear at the time what these particles were.
Thompson added two plates in the midst of the path of the cathode ray, and noticed the ray was deflected up, towards the positive plate.
This led the undertsanding the particles must be negatively charged, known today as "electrons".
So the electron was discovered in this experiment, at 1897. 10 Years layer, Thompson won a Nobel Prize for it.
The Plum Pudding model of the atom, by Thompson, claimed the atoms are spheres with no net charge.
Since electrons have a negative charge, they must be embedded in some positively charge region of the sphere (like raisins in pudding).
This model lasted for ~10 years.

In 1909 a researcher named Hans Geiger and an undergraduate named Ernest Marsden worked with Ernest Rutherford  (one of Thompson's students)
to perform an experiment to test the Plum Pudding model.
They put a sample of Radium stored in a box with a very small hole (it was known that Radium naturally decays and emits "alpha" particles).
The particles were aimed at a piece of gold foil, surrounded by a circular shaped screen made of zinc sulfide.
As they expected, for most of the experiment the particles went straight through the goil foil, but around 1 in 8000 was refelcted.
Rutherford said:
> It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life.
> It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.
Rutherford concluded that in order for the particle to bounce back there must be something small, dense, and positively charged that it interacted with.
The nucleus of the atom was discovered.
So the Rutherford model included a positively charged dense nucleus, and electrons orbiting it like planets around the sun.

During the 1850's Gustav Kirchoff developed a limited understanding of the spectral lines.
Shining a light in a laboratory, through a prism, through a gas, there were black lines in the continuous result.
Likewise, removing the light source and instead heating the gas, resulted in the reverse (colors where it was previous black).
The natural conclusion was the gas was absorbing some of the light.
Therefore, the result when the light source was active was called "absorption spectrum" (signifying what the gas absorbed)
The reverse was named an "emission spectrum" (signifying what the gas emitted)

In 1955, Johan Balmer discovered a methametical relationship between wavelength's and the specral lines.
A few years layer, the Swedish physicist Johannes Rydberg discovered the famous Rydberg formula - to calculate the observed specral lines of hydrogen.

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed a new model of the atom, being well aware of the observed hydrogen spectrum.
He was also aware of a fundemental flaw in Rutherford's model.

In the late 1800's James Clark Maxwell discovered a set of four equations that describe the behavior of electricity and magnetism.
According to Maxwell's theory, any accelerating charged particle must emit energy in the form of electromagnetic radiation.
In Rutherford's model, the electrons orbit the nucleus, changing direction continuously and thus accelerating.
Consequently they must radiate energy.
The natural result here, is the electrons must lose energy quickly and spiral into the nucleus (stable atoms should not exist)

Bohr's model consisted of four postulates:
1. electrons move in circular orbits
2. Maxwell's theory does not apply to objects as small as an atom

The next two postulates were inspired by recent work by Max Plank and Albert Einstein.
Plank proposed that the energy of light can only come in integer multiples of some discrete amount (E=hf).
Plank referred to this assumption as an act of despiration, he had no clue why it worked.
In 1905, Einstein took this further showing this idea is not a wild guess.
There really were photons (or discrete packets of light), and he used this formula to explain the photoelectric effect.

Influenced by this quantization idea, Bohr claimed the other postulates:
3. the electron can only orbit at certain fixed values, where the angular momentum of the electron is some multiple of h-bar.
4. When the electron moved between these fixed orbits, it did so by either absorbing or emitting a photon. The energy of the photon was Delta-E = hf. 

Now we can finally understand the model that was at the forefront of Heisenberg's thinking during his retreat to Heligonald.
Heisenberg knew the model must be modified in some way, as it worked well for Hydrogen, but completely failed for atoms with more than one electron.

In 1923 the situation was so dire, that Max Born claimed "The whole system of concepts of physics must be reconstructed from the ground up".

In 1924, Heisenberg went to visit Bohr in the institute for theoretical physics in Denmark.
They worked together for over a year attempting to resolve the issues of the model with little success.

Breakthrough came in the summer of 1925, while Heisenberg was working in isolation during Heligoland retreat.
He published a paper that started with:
> It seems more reasonable to completely discard unobserved quantities like the eelctron's orbit and period...
> Try to establish a theoretical quantum mechanics, analogous to classical mechanics, but in which only relations between observable quantities occur.

Heisenberg discards the entire existing model and begins from scratch.
Now since Bohr's electron orbits are not observable, what are the observable quantities?
We can't observe the orbit itself, but we can observe the transitions between orbits (or rather the effect of the transitions, the spectral lines).
Adding one frequency to another (of photon resulting from emission) always results in a third frequency, of the same atom (Rydberg-Ritz combination principles)
